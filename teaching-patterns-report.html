Two rules fall out of this. Copy length rises with how much the user chose to be there: nobody opts into a tooltip, everybody opts into a video. And no surface in the sampled set carried two concepts at once, regardless of budget.

Before signup · marketing site

The "how it works" ladder

Three or four steps, verb first, one visual each. This is where a genuinely novel concept gets its only uninterrupted explanation, and every product with an unfamiliar mechanic builds one.

Step count
3 in most cases, 4 at the ceiling. No sampled section used 5+.
Headline shape
Imperative verb + object. "Scan the code", not "Code scanning".
Visual per step
Always. Illustration for abstract steps, product shot for concrete ones.
Numbering
Explicit when order is causal, dropped when the steps are parallel benefits.

The interesting split is illustration versus screenshot. Products whose mechanic is physical or off-screen (delivery, access control, logistics) draw it, because a screenshot of the app would explain the wrong half of the product. Products whose mechanic lives entirely in software show the software.

The second split is where the ladder sits on the page. When the concept is the hard part, it appears immediately below the hero. When the concept is familiar and the differentiator is quality, it drops below the fold and the hero carries proof instead.

Minute 0 · immediately after signup

Ask before you tell

The longest onboarding in the sample was 23 screens, and almost none of it was instruction. It was questions. Personalization buys the right to teach later, because everything taught afterwards can be framed as "for you".

Screens per question
One. Multi-question screens appeared only for account fields, never for preferences.
Answer format
3–6 large tappable cards. Free text is avoided.
Progress
Always shown. Bar, dots, or "3 of 8".
Payoff screen
A summary that names what the answers changed. Skipping this wastes the whole sequence.

Uxcel runs the pattern hardest: a long, low-effort question chain where every screen is one decision, and the reward is a tailored track rather than a generic dashboard. Cohere and Vapi run shorter versions aimed at routing a developer to the right first action.

The failure mode is asking without spending. If the answers do not visibly change what the user sees next, the interview reads as data collection and the trust it was meant to buy goes negative.

Minute 1 · first load of the app

The single-idea modal

One concept, one visual, 15 to 40 words, one primary button and one way out. This is the surface that most often gets overloaded, and the products that resist overloading it are noticeably the strongest ones.

Concepts per modal
Exactly one, in every sampled case.
Visual
Looping demo or a single abstract graphic. Rarely a full screenshot.
Actions
One primary, one dismiss. A secondary "learn more" appears only when depth exists elsewhere.
Dismiss
Always available, always non-destructive. Nothing sampled trapped the user.

The best examples define a noun before they teach a verb. Where a product invents a term — a database, a workspace, a voice — the first modal exists to name that object, and the term is then used without explanation everywhere afterwards. Products that skip the naming step end up re-explaining the same idea in five different tooltips.

Note what the modal is not doing: it is not previewing the whole feature set. It handles the one thing that would otherwise make the interface unreadable, then gets out of the way.

Minute 2 · first session in the interface

Coach marks on live UI

The guided tour, done properly, is a sequence of small tooltips anchored to elements that actually exist, each one moving the user closer to a real action. Done improperly, it is a slideshow narrating a screenshot.

Steps
3–5 in the tight examples. Remote's ran to 10 and is the outer edge of tolerable.
Counter
Present in every sampled tour. "2 of 5" or a dot row.
Controls
Next, Back, and a persistent close. Back matters more than it looks.
Anchoring
Tooltip attached to the real control, with the rest of the UI dimmed rather than hidden.

Canva's four-step tour is the cleanest specimen: it opens with a short invitation, then each tooltip points at one control and says what it does in a single line. Deel wraps its tour in an explicit start card, which is the polite version — the user consents to the tour before it takes over the screen.

Length is the whole risk. A tour is a modal state the user cannot work inside, so every additional step is borrowed against attention. The products that ran long tours also ran a checklist alongside, which suggests they know the tour will be abandoned and are hedging.

Day 1 → week 1 · persistent

The resumable checklist

The single highest-value pattern in the set, because it is the only one that survives the first session. A tour is spent the moment it is dismissed. A checklist waits.

Placement
Sidebar panel, dashboard card, or a collapsible widget pinned bottom-right.
Rows
4–6. Each is a real task with a real outcome, not a lesson to read.
Progress
Explicit count or percentage. Completed rows stay visible and checked.
Retirement
Disappears or collapses on completion. Nothing sampled left a dead checklist behind.

Front's seven-screen setup list is the fullest version: the checklist is the home surface, and each row opens the real feature rather than a description of it. Better Stack does the same in a denser developer register. Monarch keeps it to a compact card, which is the right scale for a smaller product.

Checklist rows are the tersest teaching copy that works. Three to eight words in the title, a supporting line only when the task's purpose is not obvious from its name. The row itself teaches by being completed.

First real task · in the product

Learn by doing, in a sandbox

The most expensive pattern and the most effective one. Instead of describing the workflow, the product hands over a half-finished artifact with sample data and asks the user to finish it.

Prerequisite
Sample data that looks real. Lorem-ipsum sandboxes teach nothing.
Guidance
Inline and adjacent, not overlaid. The user must be able to type while reading.
Output
Something keepable. The tutorial artifact becomes the user's first real document.
Best fit
Products with a genuinely new mental model, where telling has already failed.

Hex is the clearest case: the guided tutorial runs inside a live notebook, so the concept being taught and the surface teaching it are the same object. That is the structural advantage — there is no translation step between "what I was shown" and "what I now have to do".

The tell for a weak version is a sandbox that cannot be broken. If nothing the user does has consequences, the sandbox is a slideshow with a text cursor.

Whenever a surface is empty

Empty state as teacher

The cheapest teaching surface to build, the tersest to write, and the only one that appears exactly when the user needs it. It also never expires, which makes it strictly better value than a tour.

Structure
Graphic, one-line title, one supporting line, one primary action. Nothing else.
Word count
6–18 in the body. The shortest copy anywhere in the product.
Title voice
States what is missing, not that something is missing. "No invoices yet" beats "Nothing here".
Action
The exact verb the user would have gone looking for.

Typeform and Literal are the tidiest specimens: a small illustration, a title that names the absent object, one line describing what the object is for, and a button. Workable and Bonsai add a second line where the object is genuinely unfamiliar. Klarna and PayPal show the consumer register, where the graphic does most of the explaining.

This is the pattern to over-invest in if you only have budget for one. It scales with the product, requires no orchestration layer, and cannot be dismissed into oblivion.

Empty state copy shape

You don't have any items here yet. Items are how you organise your work in the app. Once you create one it will appear in this list. Click below to get started.

On demand · never in the path

Depth, kept off the path

Every product in the set had long-form material — videos, docs, courses — and every one of them kept it behind a nav item. Nothing long was ever pushed into a first session.

Entry point
A "Learn", "Guides" or "Academy" item in the main nav or help menu.
Format
Short video cards with duration labels, grouped by task rather than by feature.
Length signal
Runtime shown on the card. This is the courtesy that makes depth acceptable.
Rule
Opt-in only. The first session links to it, never plays it.

Qatalog, Lemni, Glide and beehiiv all run the same shape: a library grid, duration badges, and task-shaped titles. The library is where verbosity is not just permitted but expected, because the user has already declared how much time they are willing to spend.

What everyone converged on

Ten things that were true in every product sampled

One idea per surface

No modal, tooltip, empty state, or checklist row in the set carried two concepts. When a product had two things to say, it used two surfaces or two steps, never one denser paragraph.

Name the object before you teach the action

Products with an invented noun spend their first surface defining it, then use the term bare everywhere after. Skipping this forces re-explanation in every subsequent tooltip.

Progress is always visible, and exit is always open

Every multi-step sequence showed position. Every one had a close. The good ones made closing non-destructive and resumable rather than final.Counter + persistent dismiss + resumable state

Point at things that exist

Strong tours anchor to the live control the user is about to click, with surrounding UI dimmed rather than replaced. Weak tours narrate a static image and leave the user to find the control afterwards.

Verb first, outcome named

Titles across every surface are imperative and describe the result, not the mechanism. "Connect your bank", not "Bank connection settings". The verb on the button matches the verb in the confirmation.

Copy length tracks how much the user opted in

Nobody chose to see a tooltip, so it gets eight words. Everybody chose to open the tutorial video, so it gets nine minutes. Verbosity is earned by consent, not by importance.

Ask, then tell, then hand over

The sequence is consistent: personalization questions, one framing modal, then real work. Products that teach before asking produce generic instruction that reads as filler.

Persistent beats one-shot

Tours are spent on dismissal. Checklists and empty states wait. Products that invested in both hedged the tour with a checklist, which tells you which one they trust.

Same words in all three places

The term used in the marketing ladder is the term in the first-run modal is the term in the empty state. Synonyms across surfaces are the most common self-inflicted comprehension cost.

Depth exists, but stays behind a door

Every product had long-form material. None of it appeared unrequested in a first session. The first session's job is to link to depth, not deliver it.

Where the strongest ones diverge

The choice is which surfaces to build, not how many

Weak onboarding builds every surface at low quality. Strong onboarding picks two or three and executes them properly. The pick correlates with how novel the product's core object is.

Reading this against PRISM

PRISM is the "physical mechanic" row and the "novel object" row at once

The concept that has to land is that one physical copy of a card serves every deck, because a stripe on the Perfect Fit inner records which deck it belongs to. That is off-screen, so a screenshot cannot teach it. And "pool" and "stripe" are invented nouns, so the app has to name them before it uses them.

That points at three surfaces, not eight. An illustrated ladder on the marketing site, empty states that name the objects, and a short resumable checklist. A coach-mark tour would be the wrong spend: it teaches interface navigation, and PRISM's hard part is not the interface.

Marketing site
A three-rung illustrated ladder: sleeve, mark, remix. Drawn, not screenshotted, because two of the three rungs happen on a table.
First load
One modal that defines pool and stripe in under 40 words, then never defines them again. Dismissible, non-destructive.
Empty states
Decks and Pool each name the absent object in one line. Cheapest surface, permanent, and it carries the vocabulary.
Checklist
Four rows, sidebar, resumable. Add first card, build first deck, mark a stripe, print a list. Retires on completion.
Behind a door
Spirit Guide alignment and double-sleeving belong in a Learn section, not in a first session. They are depth, and depth is opt-in.
Vocabulary lock
Same words on the Kickstarter page, the site ladder, the modal, and the empty states. Perfect Fit inner, stripe, pool. No synonyms.
